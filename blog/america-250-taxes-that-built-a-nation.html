America at 250: the taxes that built a nation

J Park & Associates · July 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Tomorrow the country turns 250. And it’s worth remembering, from a small CPA office on Foothill Blvd., that the whole thing started as a tax dispute. Not a grand philosophical one at first — a fight over a duty on tea, a stamp on paper, a levy imposed by a government three thousand miles away that no colonist had voted for. The Declaration signed in Philadelphia in 1776 is, among other things, one of history’s more consequential complaints about a tax bill.

We spend our days on the modern end of that story — sales-tax returns, payroll deposits, the CP14 notice on a client’s desk. So on the occasion of the semiquincentennial, here’s the long arc of it: how the American tax system got from “no taxation without representation” to the return you’ll file next spring.

“No taxation without representation” (1765–1776)

The grievance that lit the fuse wasn’t the amount — British taxes on the colonies were modest by the standards of the day. It was the principle. Parliament passed the Stamp Act of 1765, taxing everything from legal documents to playing cards, and the colonists had no seat in the room that voted for it. The Townshend duties and the Tea Act followed. In December 1773, a few dozen men dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea into Boston Harbor — a tax protest that has anchored the American imagination ever since.

The lesson embedded at the founding is one we still live under: a tax is legitimate only when the people paying it have a say through their representatives. Every ballot measure on a California sales-tax increase is that same idea, 250 years downstream.

A nation funded by tariffs (1789–1861)

Having just fought a war over taxes, the new republic was understandably wary of them. For its first century, the federal government funded itself mostly through tariffs — duties on imported goods — plus the occasional excise tax. There was no income tax and, for most people, no direct dealing with a federal tax collector at all.

The wariness didn’t mean taxes were painless. In 1791 the government put an excise on distilled spirits to pay down Revolutionary War debt, and grain farmers on the Pennsylvania frontier — who turned surplus grain into whiskey to make it worth transporting — revolted. The Whiskey Rebellion of 1794 was the first serious test of whether the young federal government could actually collect a tax it had passed. President Washington rode out at the head of the militia; it could.

The thread worth noticing

For most of American history, “taxes” meant taxes on things — imports, whiskey, tobacco, gasoline. The tax on income that dominates modern life is a relatively recent arrival. When your restaurant remits sales tax or your station files fuel excise, you’re working in the oldest branch of the American tax tree.

The first income tax — and its repeal (1861–1872)

The income tax was born of emergency. To fund the Civil War, the Revenue Act of 1861 introduced the first federal tax on personal income, refined in 1862 into a graduated tax and paired with a new agency to collect it — the office of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, ancestor of today’s IRS. It worked well enough to help pay for the war. But it was always sold as a wartime measure, and in 1872 Congress let it lapse. For a while, the country went back to funding itself the old way.

The 16th Amendment: the income tax becomes permanent (1913)

The modern era begins in 1913. A late-19th-century attempt at a peacetime income tax had been struck down by the Supreme Court as an unconstitutional “direct tax,” so reformers did the hard thing: they amended the Constitution. The 16th Amendment gave Congress the explicit power to tax income “from whatever source derived,” and later that year the first modern Form 1040 appeared — a tax that touched roughly the top 1% of earners, with a top rate of 7%.

That narrow tax on the wealthy is the same instrument that now reaches nearly every working household. The 1040 you sign each April is a direct descendant of that 1913 form — longer, and with considerably more schedules.

Withholding, and the tax that reaches everyone (1935–1943)

Two changes turned the income tax from an elite levy into a mass one. The Social Security Act of 1935 created the payroll tax that still funds Social Security and, later, Medicare — the FICA line every employer on our books knows well. Then, to finance World War II, the Current Tax Payment Act of 1943 introduced payroll withholding: tax taken out of each paycheck as it’s earned, rather than paid in one lump the following year.

Withholding is why most Americans never write a single large check to the IRS — and why, for a small-business owner who doesn’t have an employer withholding on their behalf, quarterly estimated payments exist. If you’ve ever wondered why the self-employed have to guess at four payments a year, this 1943 law is the reason.

From one page to the modern code

The century since has been a long accumulation: the standard deduction, the corporate tax and the S-corporation election, capital-gains rules, the Earned Income Tax Credit, the alternating waves of reform and complication that produced the code we work in today. California layered its own system on top — the Franchise Tax Board, the sales-and-use tax administered by what is now the CDTFA, the excise taxes that adjust every July 1. The 1913 form fit on a page. The 2026 code does not.

What this means for you

None of this history is trivia to a working business owner — it’s the architecture you operate inside. Sales tax, excise, payroll, income, estimated payments: each is a different layer laid down in a different century, and each has its own rules and deadlines. The job of a CPA is to hold all of it at once so you don’t have to. That’s as true in the firm’s 250th year of the republic as it was in its first.

So tomorrow, somewhere between the parade and the barbecue, it’s worth a small toast to the improbable thing this all is. A country that began by throwing tea in a harbor over a tax it hadn’t voted for went on to build one of the most elaborate voluntary-compliance tax systems in the world — funded, ultimately, by hundreds of millions of people and businesses filing their returns. The corner restaurant remitting sales tax, the optometrist making a quarterly estimate, the station operator filing fuel excise: that’s the system working, one small business at a time.

Happy 250th. From all of us on Foothill Blvd. — enjoy the Fourth. We’ll keep an eye on the ledger.
